A thousand years ago, at the eastern gate of Mesopotamia, a Kurdish dynasty rose to power.
The great road from Baghdad to Khorasan ran straight through their lands.
For nearly one hundred and thirty years, they held this gate.
They built palaces, led cavalry armies, and lived through alliances and wars with neighboring dynasties.
Their name was the Annazids.

East of the Tigris River, the plain of Mesopotamia meets the mountains.
Right on this threshold rises the city of Hulwan.
Known today as Sarpol-e Zahab, this city was, according to medieval geographers, the last city of the plain of Iraq, that is, of Mesopotamia.
The Alwand stream, rising at Hulwan, flows into the Diyala River, and from there into the Tigris.
This land carries a very ancient memory.
Four thousand years ago, the Lullubi king Anubanini had his victory carved into the rocks of Hulwan.
Later, the Kassites came out of this region and took the throne of Babylon.
For thousands of years, the ancestors of the Kurds lived at this gate between Mesopotamia and the mountains.
The story of the Annazids begins around the year 990.
Abu al-Fath Muhammad ibn Annaz, of the Kurdish Shadhanjan tribe, seized power in the city of Hulwan.
The dynasty took its name from his father, Annaz.
The Shadhanjan were a powerful Kurdish community living between the Mesopotamian plain and the mountains.
With the horsemen of his tribe, Abu al-Fath turned the balance of the region in his favor and ruled Hulwan for twenty years.
And so the eastern gate of Mesopotamia passed into the hands of a Kurdish emirate.
Hulwan was no ordinary city.
The great road linking Baghdad to Hamadan and Khorasan passed through here and climbed into the mountains.
Caravans laden with silk, spices, horses and cloth, pilgrim convoys from Khorasan, envoys and armies all had to pass through this gate.
The Annazids guarded the road, and in return they collected a toll.
But the dynasty's wealth did not come from the road alone.
The well-watered lands of the Diyala plain yielded wheat and barley.
The tribes spent the winter in the mild plains of Mesopotamia, and the summer in the cool mountain pastures.
This cycle of summer and winter pastures made the plain and the mountain one single homeland.
Annazid rule rested on a tribal order.
The emir was the head of the dynasty, but he drew his power from the horsemen of the Shadhanjan tribe.
The lands were shared among members of the dynasty.
The emir's brother Muhalhil governed Shahrazur, while another brother, Surkhab, governed Bandanijin.
At court, envoys were received, taxes were collected, and councils were held with the tribal chiefs.
Important decisions were made with the approval of the tribal elders.
This order reflected the structure of a Kurdish tribal tradition that endured for centuries.
The heart of Annazid power was its cavalry.
Lightly armored Kurdish riders, armed with lances and bows, moved swiftly across the plains and laid ambushes in the mountain passes.
The dynasty's most precious assets were the castles built on steep mountain slopes.
Castles such as Sirwan were both the place where the treasury was kept and the family's refuge in times of danger.
An emir defeated on the plain could withdraw to his mountain castle and gather his strength again.
That is why, even when they lost the plain, the Annazids could not easily be destroyed.
The Annazids' most powerful neighbor was also a Kurdish dynasty: the Hasanwayhids, who ruled Dinawar and its surroundings.
The two dynasties were bound by marriage ties.
Yet at the same time, they were fierce rivals.
Again and again, the two Kurdish families clashed over caravan routes and mountain passes.
When the Hasanwayhid emir Badr ibn Hasanwayh died in 1014, Hasanwayhid power collapsed.
A large part of their lands passed to the Annazids.
The dynasty's golden age came with Abu al-Shawk Faris.
Bearing the title Husam al-Dawla, this emir ruled for thirty-five years.
Under his rule, Annazid lands stretched from Hulwan to Kermanshah, from Dinawar to Shahrazur, and from Daquq to Bandanijin.
Most of these lands lay along the eastern edge of the Mesopotamian plain.
In the balance between Baghdad and Hamadan, the Annazid cavalry was a force everyone had to reckon with.
The Annazids struck a skillful balance between powerful neighbors.
To the west stood the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad.
To the northwest, the Uqaylids ruled Mosul, and to the southwest, the Mazyadids ruled at Hilla.
To the east, the Kakuyids of Isfahan laid claim to Hamadan and Dinawar.
Abu al-Shawk sometimes allied with these neighbors, and sometimes fought them.
Envoys, gifts and marriage ties were weapons as important as the sword.
Farther away, the Kurdish dynasties of the Marwanids and the Shaddadids were also on the rise in the same age.
But a wound from within weakened the dynasty.
Struggles for the throne broke out between brothers and nephews.
The rivalry between Abu al-Shawk and his brother Muhalhil split the tribes in two.
Later, Abu al-Shawk's son Sadi sought outside help against his uncle Muhalhil.
This division opened the way for a new storm coming from the east.
In the middle of the eleventh century, the Seljuks swiftly conquered the Iranian plateau.
In 1046, the Seljuk commander Ibrahim Inal marched on Hulwan.
The city was besieged, and it fell.
In the same year, Abu al-Shawk died in the mountain fortress of Sirwan.
The Annazids lost their cities on the Mesopotamian plain, but they did not give up the mountains.
The dynasty survived for seventy more years in its castles and in the mountains.
Muhalhil and the emirs descended from him ruled around Shahrazur, Daquq and Sirwan.
Around the year 1117, the Annazid dynasty left the stage of history.
This was the story of one of the last great Kurdish dynasties to rule at the eastern gate of Mesopotamia.
The historian Vladimir Minorsky called this age, when Daylamite and Kurdish dynasties rose to power, the Iranian Intermezzo.
Together with the Marwanids, the Shaddadids and the Hasanwayhids, the Annazids were a strong link in the political presence of the Kurds in the Middle Ages.
Today, the Kurds living around Sarpol-e Zahab, Shahrazur and Daquq are the heirs of this thousand-year-old story.

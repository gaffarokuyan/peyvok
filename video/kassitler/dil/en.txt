The sixteenth century BC.
Babylon, the most famous city in the world, had been sacked and left without a ruler.
The people who took the throne were neither Sumerians nor Akkadians.
They had come down from the Zagros mountains, a people known for their horses.
They ruled Babylon for more than four hundred years.
It was the longest dynasty in Babylonian history.
They exchanged letters with the pharaohs of Egypt, gave daughters to their kings, and asked them for gold.
Their name was the Kassites.
In other words, the ancient ancestors of the Kurds.

The homeland of the Kassites was the central Zagros mountains, rising east of the Tigris River.
Today, Kurds and Lurs live in this region.
The Kassites first appear in history in the time of Samsu-iluna, son of Hammurabi, king of Babylon.
In the Babylonian annals, one year was named like this: the year the Kassite army was defeated.
So even then, the Kassites stood at the gates of Mesopotamia.
The greatest strength of the Kassites was their horses.
The horses and war chariots they raised on the plateaus of the Zagros made them one of the fastest armies of their age.
Many terms for horse breeding in Babylonian texts came from the Kassite language.
In 1595 BC, the Hittite king Mursili the First marched all the way to Babylon and sacked the city.
The dynasty founded by Hammurabi collapsed.
The Hittites went home, and Babylon was left without a ruler.
It was the Kassites who filled this void.
Kassite kings took the throne of Babylon and ruled for about four hundred and thirty years.
The Babylonian King List counts thirty-six Kassite kings.
It is the longest reign of a single dynasty in the history of Mesopotamia.
The Kassites also gave the country their own name.
In their time, Babylon was known as Karduniash.
Some Kurdish writers see in this name the traces of the names Kardu and Kurd.
The Kassites did not destroy Babylon.
On the contrary, they united scattered city-states into a single country and ruled southern Mesopotamia in peace for centuries.
The Kassite king Agum recorded in his inscription that he brought back the statue of Marduk, which the Hittites had carried off from Babylon.
In this way, he won the hearts of the people of Babylon.
In the fourteenth century BC, the world was in the hands of five great powers: Egypt, the Hittites, Mitanni, Assyria and Kassite Babylon.
These kings addressed one another as brother.
The Amarna Letters, found in Egypt, have preserved this diplomacy to this day.
In a letter to the pharaoh Akhenaten, the Kassite king Burnaburiash complains: I put the forty minas of gold you sent into the furnace, and not even ten minas came out.
The Kassite kings formed marriage alliances with the pharaohs of Egypt.
Kadashman-Enlil sent his daughter as a bride to Pharaoh Amenhotep the Third.
This people who came down from the Zagros now sat at the same table as the most powerful kings in the world.
The trade network of Kassite Babylon stretched from the lapis lazuli mines of Afghanistan to Dilmun in the Persian Gulf.
Dilmun, today's Bahrain, was governed by a Kassite governor.
The Kassites became the guardians of Mesopotamian civilization.
King Kurigalzu founded the city of Dur-Kurigalzu, bearing his own name, near today's Baghdad.
The ziggurat of this city still stands today.
The Kassites rebuilt the temples of ancient cities such as Nippur and Uruk.
The standard texts of great works such as the Epic of Gilgamesh were compiled in this period.
The Kassites also brought a new tradition to Mesopotamia: boundary stones called kudurru.
Kings had the lands they granted inscribed on these stones, with the symbols of the gods carved upon them.
The Kassites honored the gods of Babylon, but they did not forget their own mountain gods.
Shuqamuna and Shumaliya were the guardian gods of the Zagros mountains.
Thousands of tablets from the Kassite period were found in the excavations at Nippur.
These tablets describe daily life, from tax records to the care of horses.
Kassite craftsmen also mastered the art of glassmaking.
In the twelfth century BC, Elam, the neighbor to the east, went on the attack.
The Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte sacked Babylon and carried the famous stele inscribed with the Laws of Hammurabi off to the city of Susa.
That stele is displayed today in the Louvre Museum in Paris.
Around 1155 BC, the last Kassite king, Enlil-nadin-ahi, lost his throne.
The Kassite dynasty in Babylon came to an end.
But the Kassites did not disappear.
Having lost the throne of Babylon, they withdrew to their homeland, the Zagros mountains.
For centuries, Assyrian kings led campaigns into the Kassite land.
The Assyrian king Sennacherib recorded in his inscriptions that in 702 BC he marched into the mountain homeland of the Kassites.
A thousand years later, Greek historians wrote of the same people under the name Kossaioi.
Strabo writes that the Kossaioi were skilled archers.
Arrian tells how Alexander the Great led a winter campaign against them.
Even the Persian kings paid the Kossaioi a toll when crossing the mountain road between Susa and Ecbatana.
Once again, the Zagros mountains had protected a people.
Today, the Kurdish and Lur tribes living in the same valleys of the Zagros carry on this mountain life that has lasted for thousands of years.
The Kassites were not alone in these lands.
The Gutians, Lullubi, Hurrians, Mitanni and Medes also lived to the north and east of Mesopotamia.
According to many historians, these peoples are the ancient layers that formed the Kurds.
Foremost among those who hold this view are foreign scholars.
The American Assyriologist Ephraim Speiser, in his work Mesopotamian Origins, counts the Kassites, together with the Gutians and the Lullubi, among the native peoples of the Zagros.
Arshak Safrastian, in his book Kurds and Kurdistan, examines the peoples of the Zagros as the ancestors of the Kurds.
Mehrdad Izady presents the Kassites as one of the most important layers forming the Kurdish people, and looks for the traces of Kassite mountain traditions in Kurdish society today.
The Kurdish historian Muhammad Amin Zaki Beg also counts the Kassites among the ancient ancestors of the Kurds.
Of course, there are opposing views as well.
The main reason is that very few documents in the Kassite language have survived.
All we have is a bilingual glossary and the names of kings.
Yet geography, mountain life and a continuity of thousands of years make this bond strong.
A people who came down from the Zagros mountains ruled Babylon, the most famous city in the world, for four hundred years.
They sat at the same table as the pharaohs, protected the civilization of Mesopotamia, and in the end returned once more to their mountains.
The story of the Kassites is one of the oldest pages of Kurdish history.

Four thousand two hundred years ago, a people from east of Mesopotamia stepped onto the stage of history: the Gutians.
They brought Akkad, the world's first empire, to its knees and ruled Mesopotamia, the cradle of civilization, for a full century.
Sumerian scribes gave them a name in fear: the Dragon of the Mountains.
So who were these Gutians?
How did they rule Mesopotamia, and why do so many historians count them among the oldest ancestors of the Kurds?

The land between the Tigris and the Euphrates witnessed the greatest inventions in human history.
The first cities, writing, the wheel and written law were all born here.
Sumerian cities such as Uruk, Ur, Lagash and Nippur were the world's first centers of civilization.
In the twenty-third century BC, Sargon of Akkad conquered these cities one by one and founded the first known empire in history.
The Akkadian Empire stretched from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean.
Sargon's grandson Naram-Sin called himself king of the four corners of the world.
But the end of this great empire would come from the east.
Mesopotamian sources call the land east of the Tigris, between the Lesser Zab and the Diyala rivers, Gutium.
This was the homeland of the Gutians, and the roads descending to the Mesopotamian plain were under their control.
Under Naram-Sin's son Shar-kali-sharri, the struggle between Akkad and the Gutians intensified.
On Akkadian tablets, years were named after the important events that took place in them.
One of these year names reads: "The year Shar-kali-sharri captured Sharlag, king of Gutium."
But Akkad's victories did not last.
The empire was shaken by revolts, struggles for the throne and a great drought.
After Shar-kali-sharri's death, Akkad fell into such chaos that the Sumerian King List describes the period with a mocking question.
"Who was king, who was not king?"
The famous Sumerian text "The Curse of Agade" blames Akkad's fall on the wrath of the gods, and the people sent to carry out that wrath are named.
The Gutians.
The text describes them as a people who could not be controlled.
Around 2150 BC the Akkadian Empire collapsed, and the royal cities of Mesopotamia passed into the hands of the Gutians.
The Sumerian King List contains a plain record.
"Kingship passed to the army of Gutium."
The list describes the first period of the Gutians with a curious sentence.
"In the army of Gutium there was at first no known king; they were their own kings."
Many scholars read this sentence as a sign that the Gutians were governed not by a single absolute ruler but by a shared council or by tribal leaders.
According to the list, twenty-one Gutian kings reigned for about a hundred years.
Most of their kings stayed on the throne for only a few years.
This suggests that leadership did not pass from father to son but changed hands among the tribes.
The Gutians governed the Sumerian cities through local governors.
Lugal-annatum, governor of the city of Umma, dated his own inscription "in the time of Si'um, king of Gutium."
In other words, the cities kept their own administrations but recognized the sovereignty of the Gutian king.
The Gutians also took over Akkad's language of government and its traditions.
A mace head belonging to King La'erabum reads, in Akkadian, "the mighty king of Gutium."
The most remarkable of these kings is Erridupizir.
In his inscriptions in Nippur, the holiest city of Mesopotamia, he introduces himself like this: "King of Gutium, king of the four corners of the world."
This is the very title used by the Akkadian emperors.
His inscriptions describe his campaigns against rebellious regions and how he dedicated the spoils to the temple of the god Enlil.
Under the Gutians, the Sumerian cities also enjoyed a brilliant era.
Lagash, under Prince Gudea, built temples and lived through a golden age of art.
So the Gutians were not raiders who plundered and withdrew.
They were rulers who inherited the imperial legacy of Mesopotamia.
Leaders chosen by council, a confederation of tribes and local autonomy...
This is exactly the picture we will meet thousands of years later in the social structure of the Kurds.
About a hundred years later, Utu-hengal, king of Uruk, launched a great revolt against the Gutians.
In his victory inscription he speaks of the Gutians like this: "Gutium, the fanged serpent of the mountains, the dragon of the mountains."
At that time the last Gutian king, Tirigan, was on the throne.
According to the Sumerian King List, Tirigan reigned for only forty days.
Utu-hengal marched north with his army.
He defeated and captured the two commanders Tirigan sent against him.
At the end of the war, Tirigan abandoned his army and fled to a city called Dubrum.
But the people of Dubrum seized him and handed him over to Utu-hengal.
Soon after, Ur-Nammu founded a new dynasty in the city of Ur, but the struggle did not end.
According to some later Sumerian texts, even Ur-Nammu lost his life in a battle against the Gutians.
The political rule of the Gutians in Mesopotamia had come to an end.
But the Gutians did not disappear.
For the next fifteen hundred years, the name Gutium never vanished from Mesopotamian sources.
Hammurabi, king of Babylon, recorded his victories over the army of Gutium.
Assyrian kings led campaigns into the land of Qutu.
When the Persian king Cyrus the Great took Babylon in 539 BC, he listed Gutium among the lands under his rule on the Cyrus Cylinder.
And the first commander to enter Babylon was Ugbaru, governor of Gutium.
So for seventeen hundred years the name Gutium went on describing the people east of Mesopotamia.
The Gutians were not the only ancient people living in these lands.
The Lullubi, the Hurrians, the Kassites and the Medes also lived to the north and east of Mesopotamia.
According to many historians, these peoples, together with the Gutians, are the layers that formed the Kurds.
Foremost among those who voiced this link most strongly are foreign historians.
The orientalist Arshak Safrastian, working in England, sees the Gutians directly as the ancestors of the Kurds in his book published in 1948, and links the name Guti to the root of the name Kurd.
The chain of names is truly striking.
Assyrian records mention names such as Qurti and Kurti for the peoples of this region.
The Greek commander Xenophon writes of the warlike Kardouchoi living north of the Tigris.
In Roman times the same region was known as Corduene.
G. R. Driver of Oxford University studied how the name Kurd relates to these ancient names.
Vladimir Minorsky emphasized the role of the Medes in the formation of the Kurds.
Ephraim Speiser described the Gutians as the native people of the lands east of Mesopotamia.
Mehrdad Izady argues that the Gutians and the other ancient peoples form the foundation of the Kurdish people.
The Kurdish historian Muhammad Amin Zaki Beg holds the same view.
Of course, there are opposing views as well.
The main reason is that very few documents in the Gutian language have survived.
All we have are a few names of kings.
Yet geography, social structure and the continuity of names over thousands of years make this thesis strong.
The plains of Mesopotamia were rich but defenseless.
Empires came, cities were burned.
In the thirteenth century the Mongols razed Baghdad and all of Mesopotamia.
But thanks to the strategic advantage of the mountains, the Kurds were not hit by this destruction as hard as the peoples of the plains.
The Kurds have an ancient saying: Ji çiyan pê ve, hevalên me tune.
We have no friends but the mountains.
The Gutians were the people who brought down Mesopotamia's first empire and inherited its legacy.
One of the first pages of Kurdish history was written right here, in these lands.
